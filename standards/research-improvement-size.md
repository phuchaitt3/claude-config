# How large an improvement counts in NLP results

- **Question:** Is there a rule of thumb for how many points (or %) of improvement is acceptable or good in an NLP/ML result, and is a modest overall gain diluted by a weak upstream stage (e.g. retrieval in RAG) normal to report?
- **Finding:**
  - No accepted threshold exists. The NLP methodology literature judges a gain by whether it is reliable, not by a fixed size: a paired significance test on the same test set (paired bootstrap or approximate randomisation are standard), and consistency across seeds, datasets or models. Dror et al. (ACL 2018) give a protocol for choosing the test and find many ACL/TACL 2017 papers skipped or misapplied it. Berg-Kirkpatrick et al. (EMNLP 2012) show larger gains tend to be more significant, that a bigger test set does not remove the need for a test, and that significance on one test set predicts poorly on data from another domain.
  - Size is relative to the setting: the baseline's headroom, the cost of the method (training-free inference-time methods are compared with other training-free methods), and the model size. A gain is usually reported in absolute points ("+2.7 F1"), with relative change optional and labelled.
  - Splitting results by upstream quality is standard. Inference-time RAG decoding papers (e.g. Adaptive Contrastive Decoding, EMNLP Findings 2024) report gold-context and noisy-context settings separately, because a generation-side method cannot recover evidence retrieval missed, so the overall gain is diluted by retrieval failures. Reporting both the full-set number and the "retrieval found the answer" subset is normal, provided the subset is defined before looking at the method's results and the full-set number is shown too.
  - *Checker's inference:* gains of a few F1 points on a QA benchmark are ordinary for training-free decoding and attention methods, and a reviewer reads them as credible when backed by a paired test, several runs and the conditional breakdown. A single run with no test leaves "is it noise?" open. On a resume, the full-set number is the honest headline. The subset number is a strength when stated with its condition, and its explanation belongs in the interview.
- **Sources:**
  - Dror, Baumer, Shlomov, Reichart, "The Hitchhiker's Guide to Testing Statistical Significance in NLP", ACL 2018 (peer-reviewed): https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/
  - Berg-Kirkpatrick, Burkett, Klein, "An Empirical Investigation of Statistical Significance in NLP", EMNLP-CoNLL 2012 (peer-reviewed): https://aclanthology.org/D12-1091/
  - Kim et al., "Adaptive Contrastive Decoding in Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Handling Noisy Contexts", Findings of EMNLP 2024 (peer-reviewed): https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.136.pdf
- **Limits:** Read through abstracts and search summaries, not full papers. No source gives a numeric "good gain" threshold, and none studies how resume readers or hiring managers judge effect sizes. The point about decoding-method gains being a few points is the checker's inference from the field, not a survey.
