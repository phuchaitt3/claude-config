# Entry-level AI engineer: how hard, and the common routes to a first job

- **Question:** How hard is it to get an entry-level AI engineering role, and what are the most common ways to get the first AI engineer job?
- **Finding:**
  - *Difficulty:* hard, and harder than the general software market. Sources agree on direction, not on exact numbers: one analysis of about 1,000 AI postings found 2.5% target 0-2 years of experience; a job-board count of US AI/ML engineer postings put entry-level at about 6%. LinkedIn data (as reported by Forbes) shows new-grad hiring down 20% from Dec 2019 to Apr 2026, engineering majors down 25%. Another tracker reports US entry-level postings down 35% since early 2023. Senior share of IT postings rose (38.8% to 43.1% year on year) while entry-level fell (8.1% to 7.4%). Some employers hire juniors (IBM reportedly announced tripled entry-level hiring in early 2026, including AI engineering).
  - *Routes:* no survey of how people land a first AI engineer job was found. The routes the sources describe, in the order they recur:
    1. Start as a software engineer (or take an internship), then move to AI work. An internal transfer to the company's first AI team is called much easier than switching companies (Pragmatic Engineer interview).
    2. Build and publish LLM-based projects (GitHub, hackathons) as proof; the AI/LLM-engineer role (building features on existing models) is the easier entry than the ML-engineer role (training, fine-tuning, serving; more math and GPU work).
    3. Combine AI skills with domain knowledge, which one roadmap says beats a generalist with the same skills.
    4. Standard software-engineering interview preparation. The first check found one interview saying there is no distinct "AI engineer interview"; the second check (below) found most 2026 guides describe a loop with LLM-specific parts added to the usual coding round, so that claim is only partly right: coding stays, LLM topics are added.
  - *AI/LLM engineer vs ML engineer (second check, 2026-11-13):*
    - Sources agree AI engineering is the more accessible entry and more portfolio-driven. Typical people: backend or full-stack engineers moving into AI. ML engineering has the higher formal barrier: CS, statistics or research background, often 2-4 years as a software engineer first.
    - Maths: AI roles want linear algebra basics and probability intuition; ML roles want calculus, statistics, optimisation, ML theory.
    - Strongest portfolio signal for AI roles: a deployed RAG or LLM app with documented evaluation numbers (one guide claims this often beats a master's thesis; vendor claim, unverified). For ML roles: deployed, monitored models and pipelines (PyTorch, MLflow, cloud ML platforms).
    - Market counts conflict: one May 2026 analysis found ML engineer postings outnumbered AI engineer postings, 4,781 to 4,091, so "AI engineer" is not clearly the larger market. Junior pay estimates conflict across sources.
    - One guide says 6-12 months to become hireable as an ML engineer, faster for AI engineering from zero; single vendor, not established.
  - *AI engineer interview loop (second check):* guides agree on four parts:
    1. Coding: usually still at least one round of data-structures-and-algorithms problems, plus AI-flavoured tasks (wrap an LLM call with retries, chunk a document).
    2. LLM fundamentals: tokens, embeddings, tool calling, fine-tuning trade-offs, hallucination mitigation (grounding, citations, "I don't know", faithfulness evals).
    3. LLM system design, the most common hard round (45-60 minutes): design a RAG assistant, support agent, extraction pipeline or code-review bot; follow-ups on chunking, stale or conflicting context, debugging by stage (did retrieval find the passage, then did generation use it), what to log (inputs, retrieved context, output, latency, cost, eval scores) and how to catch regressions.
    4. Behavioural, plus a deep dive on one project you shipped. Take-homes of 1-7 days, often a RAG or agent build, are common.
    - Prep advice recurring in guides: one small RAG app with a small eval set; two system-design prompts practised aloud with a timer; cost and latency estimates for a real project.
  - *Are most entry jobs residencies or internships? (third check, 2026-11-13):* **no data found** on what share of entry-level AI hiring goes through residencies, internships or ordinary new-grad postings; the claim "most entry jobs are residencies" is unsupported either way. What exists:
    - Residencies are a real route but are named programmes with few places, mostly research-leaning: OpenAI (6 months, paid, full-time offers for strong performers, rolling cohorts), Meta AI (about 1 year, research training), Apple (1 year, ML), and small labs such as Lila Sciences. Several seek people without a prior ML background.
    - In Vietnam, structured programmes exist alongside ordinary fresher postings: an AI residency with a hiring pathway (Qualcomm Vietnam, Engineering Residency, 12 months), a 12-week residency at VNG with possible full-time offers, a 6-month Viettel Digital Talent AI Engineer track (April-October 2026, a theory phase then 3 months on real projects; from a third-party upload, not Viettel's site), VinAI trainee and internship schemes (no details found), and FPT R&D fresher postings asking for Python or C/C++, PyTorch, OpenCV, Linux, Docker. A guide says product and R&D employers prioritise HUST, VNU-UET and HCMUT graduates; FPT University has mandatory internships at FPT Group.
    - Our inference only: in Vietnam, structured programmes (residency, trainee, internship-to-hire) look like a main visible entry route for AI specifically because AI roles rarely appear as plain junior postings (see the posting-share figures above), but this is not measured. Nothing found compares conversion rates or counts of programme places against fresher postings.
  - Our inference: "AI engineer" is often a software-engineering job with LLM tooling, so a software-engineering entry point is the common first job and the AI title comes later.
- **Evidence strength:** *anecdotal* for routes (blogs, course providers, one interview); *sample or survey* for difficulty, but from job-board and recruiting vendors with unstated methods, so treat percentages loosely.
