# Agent judgement of model and effort per task

**Question:** How reliable is a coding agent's judgement of which model (smaller or larger) and effort level a task needs, and what does Anthropic advise for the choice?

**Finding:**

- **Vendor advice (Anthropic, Claude Code, 2026-07-07):** "Choose smaller models for more routine tasks and larger models for more complex or ambiguous tasks." Routine means precisely described edits, mechanical changes, or questions about code already in context. Hard means subtle bugs, unfamiliar domains, or architecture decisions. Use the model's default effort for most tasks and treat effort as "a general preference rather than a task-by-task decision". Check context and scoping first. If Claude had the right context, clearly tried and was still wrong, move to a larger model. If the work on a larger model is routine, drop to a smaller one. On hard multi-step tasks a larger model may finish in fewer steps, so it can cost less per task despite a higher price per token, and it can finish tasks a smaller model cannot finish at any effort. The article does not describe the agent choosing its own model.
- **Effort defaults and levels (Claude Code model-config docs, read 2026-11-15):** Fable 5.2, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 default to `medium`, Opus 4.7 to `xhigh`, and the other models to `high`. `medium` on Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 "fits day-to-day engineering work with a clear scope, such as implementing a new feature". `high` is for "work where verification matters or edge cases are likely, such as fixing a bug in an existing codebase". `max` "may show diminishing returns and is prone to overthinking". At higher levels Claude tested more edge cases and verified more of its work. At lower levels it returned a starting point sooner.
- **Agents overrate their own chance of success.** Every LLM tested by Barkan et al. (2025-12) was overconfident about its own success. Most predicted it better than chance. Larger or newer models did not discriminate better, except Claude models, which showed an improving trend. Reasoning models were no better. Overconfidence grew as agentic tasks went on. In-context records of past failures reduced it for some models but not all.
- **Coding agents specifically:** Kaddour et al. (2026-02) found overconfidence in every setting tested, for example one agent that succeeded 22% of the time predicted 77%. A prediction made before execution discriminated successes from failures at least as well as a review after execution, though the difference was not always significant. Framing the self-assessment as bug-finding gave the best calibration.
- **Routing research:** learned routers trained on outcome or preference data beat random routing. RouteLLM reports more than 2x lower cost at little quality loss on MT Bench, MMLU and GSM8K. When a model judges a query's difficulty itself, the work reports results that are useful but imperfect. Papers note that the difficulty estimate, not the router design, is the main bottleneck, and probes on internal activations often do better than asking the model.
- **Speed on routine tasks (added 2026-11-22).** On small, well-scoped tasks Opus 5.5 is slower than Sonnet 5.5 for the same result. It is not much slower in every setting. One Claude Code benchmark ran 48 runs: 4 small tasks on one 11-file PHP repo, 3 repeats each. Both models at `medium` solved 12/12. Median API time per task was 13.4 s for Sonnet and 21.8 s for Opus, and Opus cost 2.6x as much ($1.91 vs $0.73). Sonnet at `high` (14.3 s) gained nothing over `medium`. Artificial Analysis measured time per task at `low` effort as 2.6 min for Sonnet and 3.1 min for Opus, with cost per task $0.51 vs $0.98. At `medium` the times were about 3.8 vs 6.7 min (column mapping inferred from an unlabelled table). On 129 harder Go backend tasks (Requesty), Opus took 8.5 s per turn and Sonnet 4.5 s. Opus took more turns per task (18.6 vs 13), and both averaged 9.5 min per task. The table records both turn counts but does not explain how the per-task times match. Opus solved more tasks overall (92.2% vs 87.6%), with the whole gap on the hardest tasks: extra hard 86.7% vs 70%, medium both 100%, easy Sonnet 100% vs Opus 92.9%. Anthropic rates Opus 5.5's latency "Moderate" and Sonnet 5.5's "Fast". Anthropic's guidance says "dropping down will increase speed and typically reduce cost without impacting the quality of the output" once the work has been routine for a while.
- **Overthinking is a known failure of long reasoning, not specific to Opus.** Studies of reasoning models find many times more tokens on easy problems with little or no accuracy gain. Examples: about 1,953% more tokens on trivial math (arXiv 2412.21187), about 18x the tokens of standard models with sometimes lower accuracy (LLMThinkBench, arXiv 2507.04023), and a tendency to overthink easy problems while underthinking hard ones (arXiv 2505.00127). Anthropic says it targets overthinking in training and that `max` effort "is prone to overthinking". Effort, not the model, is the main lever for thinking length. Several write-ups note that effort often matters more than the choice of model, so compare models only at matched effort.
- **Vendor defaults still point to Opus.** The Claude Code docs set Opus 5.5 as the default model on Pro, Max, Team, Enterprise and the API. The models overview says "If you're unsure which model to use, start with Claude Opus 5.5 for most workloads". The `opusplan` alias uses Opus in plan mode and Sonnet for execution. Two third-party benchmarks recommend the opposite for coding agents: start on Sonnet for well-scoped work and keep Opus for ambiguous, long or hardest tasks. Anthropic's own Claude Code article also says to pick the smaller model when the work is routine.
- **(inference: findings/agent-model-effort-selection)** For a daily coding agent the evidence supports the claim "Sonnet by default, Opus when the task is hard or Sonnet is stuck". The cost and speed gain on routine work is consistent across sources. The quality gain from Opus shows up only on the hardest tasks. The general vendor default of Opus does not contradict this, because it is advice for users who have not judged the task. "Only when stuck" is the narrower form. Anthropic's article and the Requesty data also support choosing Opus up front for a task known to be hard, ambiguous or architectural, since a failed Sonnet attempt costs time before the escalation.
- **(inference: findings/agent-model-effort-selection)** Since agents overrate their own success, an agent asked "does this need the larger model?" will more often answer "no" than the outcome justifies. A rule that also puts the burden of proof on the larger model adds to that lean. The sources do not test this combination directly.

**Evidence strength:** *vendor or single source* for the Anthropic guidance and for each speed benchmark. *Common practice* for "Sonnet is faster and cheaper on routine coding tasks at equal quality": three independent measurements agree in direction. *Common practice* for "LLM agents are overconfident about their own success": several independent preprints agree. No study measures how well an agent picks between models for its own coding tasks.

**Limits:** the research items are preprints, mostly on math and coding benchmarks; none checked for peer review. Only the abstracts of the two overconfidence papers were read, so their numbers are limited to what the abstracts give. No source studies a subscription plan's usage limits, or how much a failed first attempt on the smaller model costs before escalating. The Anthropic article targets current models and may change with each model release. The speed benchmarks are small, use one repository or one private suite each, and report run-to-run spread of up to about 55% on the same task. The Artificial Analysis and Requesty cost figures at `max` effort disagree, so this entry does not use them. The overthinking papers test math benchmarks and open reasoning models, not Claude in coding agents.
