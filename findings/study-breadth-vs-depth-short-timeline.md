# Breadth versus depth, and understanding versus memorising, on a short timeline

- **Question:** With a few days before a test, is it better to cover many topics shallowly or a few with understanding, and does learning with understanding make facts stay for a learner who is bad at rote memorisation? Separate from `findings/effective-study-techniques` because that entry answers which techniques work and how to space them; this one answers how to split a fixed time between breadth and depth, and what the research says about understanding versus memorising.
- **Finding:**
  - *Breadth versus depth under a time limit: a middle setting, deeper where the learner can tell what is tested.* A Cognitive Science paper ("Optimal Learning Under Time Constraints", vol. 46) held the number of study trials fixed and varied how many items got repeated retrieval (depth) against how many items were touched (breadth). Its simulation found medium depth with medium breadth suited most situations; a narrow, deep strategy won only when the learner forecast well which items the test would ask. A 2020 PNAS model of the breadth-depth dilemma (Moreno-Bote and colleagues, as reported by the search summary) says that with small capacity, one sample per alternative (breadth) is optimal. Both are simulations or word-list experiments, not exam studies.
  - *Depth on a topic helps later grades, in a school setting.* A 2009 study (reported by Education Week) found US students who spent a month or more on one major topic in high-school science earned higher college science grades than peers who covered more topics in the same period. The authors say it does not settle how much depth is right. Different population and horizon (years, not days).
  - *Understanding helps retention and transfer, but mostly through explaining and connecting, not through length of study.* Self-explanation prompts gave a benefit in a meta-analysis of 64 studies (Bisra and colleagues 2018); the benefit was smaller when learners explained their own state of knowledge instead of the content. A ten-technique meta-analysis (Donoghue and Hattie 2021) rated elaborative interrogation and self-explanation as moderate utility, stronger when the explanation is precise, when prior knowledge is higher, and when the learner produces it; it also notes these methods suit surface facts more than deep understanding. A Deans for Impact summary says explaining relationships and building mental models lightens working-memory load. No source found isolates "understood" against "rote" on long-term retention directly.
  - *Failing first, then reading the answer, beats reading longer.* In five experiments (Richland, Kornell and Kao 2009) answering questions on a passage before reading it gave better posttest scores than extra study time, even counting only items answered wrongly in the pretest; Kornell, Hays and Bjork (2009) got the same with word pairs that guesses nearly always missed. A 2020 study found the pretest benefit for related but not for unrelated pairs, so the question has to touch something the learner partly knows.
  - *Self-made material helps more than read material, but supplied material can cost less.* The generation effect is reported as robust for recall and recognition in a 2023 meta-analysis of text generation, with a size that varies. A 1978 study found reviewing one's own notes gave better recall than reviewing a lecturer's summary. A 2025 study found no quiz difference between group-made and instructor-given notes; one source argues supplied notes cut the encoding cost so a student can focus on understanding. The benefit depends on the test matching what was generated (procedural account). Search summaries only. Not tested: time cost of editing a supplied page against writing one's own.
  - *Retrieval practice transfers to application and inference questions, unevenly.* A meta-analysis of 192 effect sizes (reported through a search summary) found a medium positive effect on transfer, including inference and application; a university-lecture study found no transfer to content studied but not practised; a Dutch thesis is titled "Limited Effectiveness of Retrieval Practice for the Transfer of Knowledge". Feedback seems to carry part of the benefit.
  - *Inference (checker's own, not in the sources):* for a learner who forgets rote facts, the cheapest "understanding" is one self-produced "why" line per fact (a precise explanation of the mechanism or the consequence), attempted before reading the page's reason, then checked; this is self-explanation plus a pretest, kept at the surface-fact level the test asks. It would give breadth at medium depth rather than choosing between them. No source tests this procedure, and none measured whether agent-written pages that the learner edits beat pages the learner writes.
- **Evidence strength:** *sample or survey* and *common practice* for self-explanation, pretesting and the generation effect (several studies and meta-analyses agree, mostly classroom and lab word or text material); *vendor or single source* for the depth-breadth simulation (one paper, one simulation); *anecdotal* for anything about coding tests. No source tests this question on interview or coding-test preparation.
