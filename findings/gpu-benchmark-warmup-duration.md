# How long a laptop GPU benchmark must run

- **Question:** How long must a laptop GPU benchmark (speed, temperature, power at one clock setting) run before the reading is trustworthy, and is a fixed 10-20 minutes per setting standard?
- **Checked:** 2026-11-17; steady-state criteria re-checked 2026-11-18
- **Evidence strength:** *vendor or single source* for the warm-up lengths (a GPU review site, a laptop maker's support page, an AI-benchmarking blog); *official docs* for NVIDIA's GEMM methodology (no fixed time) and *peer-reviewed* for the MLPerf rules (60 s minimum, which is a run length, not a thermal warm-up); *sample* (one machine, one run) for the measured plateau below. Added 2026-11-18: *secondary* summaries of US Army and munitions test procedures (the MIL-STD-810 text itself was not read); *vendor* for 3DMark's stress-test rule; *peer-reviewed* (process-control literature, through abstracts and a tutorial) for statistical steady-state tests; *sample* (one laptop, three runs on 2026-11-18) for the temperature swing.

## Finding

- **No fixed standard time; the common rule is to warm up until the readings plateau.** NVIDIA's CUTLASS measurement guidelines give no number: warm up until clocks stabilize, and for fixed-power tests until power draw settles. A laptop maker's support page says a GPU reaches thermal saturation within a few minutes of full load. A GPU review site warms the card at least 15 minutes before every test because a cold card can briefly beat a warmed one. An AI-benchmarking blog suggests a 5-minute warm-up and calls 20 minutes from cold the minimum for a steady-state run, treating minutes 5-10 as transient. These disagree because cooling designs differ; the sources are not laptop-specific standards.
- **MLPerf sets run length and conditions, not a warm-up.** MLPerf Power measures at least 60 s (a shorter workload loops until then) and averages power over valid samples. MLPerf Mobile requires at least 60 s and 1,024 samples in single-stream mode, a 20-25 °C room, a break of 0-5 minutes between tests and 10 minutes between repeated suite runs.
- **Measured on the checked laptop (GTX 1660 Ti, clock capped at 1350 MHz, Ollama qwen2.5:3b back-to-back, 2026-11-17):** GPU temperature was 59 °C at minute 1 and stayed within 60.2-61.8 °C (five-minute means) over 20 minutes; speed was 72.6 tok/s in the first five requests and 71.5 in the last five (1.5% apart). The plateau came within 1-2 minutes at that clock. An uncapped run starts hotter and may take longer; not measured.
- **Stopping when stable is the established method in software benchmarking, with known weaknesses.** Georges et al. (OOPSLA 2007) call the run steady once the coefficient of variation of the last k iterations falls below a threshold (a follow-up used 2% over 10 iterations); Laaber et al. compare variants using confidence-interval width and divergence measures; newer work uses change-point detection. Barrett et al. (2017) found the heuristic can declare steady state where none exists, and that many benchmarks never settle, so a maximum time and a "not stable" label are needed. These are for software warm-up (JIT, caches), not heat; the checker transfers the idea to temperature and speed.
- **Environmental test standards define thermal stability as a slow rate of change, not a narrow band.** US Army test procedure TOP 5-2-582 and the munitions procedure JOTP-011 call an item stable when its slowest part (largest mass, longest thermal lag) changes by no more than 2 °C per hour; a test lab's summary of MIL-STD-810 gives the same figure (another of its pages says 2 °C per 30 minutes; the standard itself was not read). These are hours-long chamber tests, so the number does not transfer; the form does: a limit on the trend, measured on the slowest-responding reading.
- **3DMark's stress test uses a relative, scale-free criterion with a fixed run.** It loops one scene 20 times (about 10 minutes) and passes when the slowest loop's frame rate is at least 97% of the fastest loop's; a drop after the GPU reaches its peak temperature (thermal throttling) fails it. The criterion is a ratio, so it works on any machine without retuning; the run length is fixed, not adaptive.
- **Process control detects steady state with tests that do not need the noise level as an input.** Cao and Rhinehart (1995) compare two estimates of the signal's variance (one from successive differences, one from a filtered mean); the ratio is near 1 at steady state whatever the noise, and the threshold is a critical value of an F-like statistic, chosen as a significance level. Later methods use a Dickey-Fuller test (window size and significance level are the only inputs) or kernel methods for performance time series. These are the established way to avoid a hand-set band in sensor units.
- **Measured on the checked laptop, 2026-11-18 (same model, same cooling, AC power):** after 8 minutes at 1200 and 1350 MHz the GPU temperature still spanned 3-4 °C within the last 60 s (read in whole degrees; the checker attributes the swing to fan cycling, not measured), while speed held; tokens/s varied by 0.3-1.1% (coefficient of variation) within 20 s. A rule of "at most 1 °C (then 2 °C) between maximum and minimum over 60 s" was never met in 8 minutes at 1200 and 1350 MHz, in two runs. A band on the swing measured the fan, not the warm-up.
- **Checker's synthesis (inference, 2026-11-18):** fixed absolute thresholds in sensor units (°C, a minimum and maximum time) are tuned to one machine and its fan; they do not carry to another machine, cooling setup or workload. The sources point to three portable choices: limit the drift of the mean (a trend), not the swing; express the limit relative to the signal's own measured noise, or as a statistical test with a significance level (Cao-Rhinehart, Dickey-Fuller), so the remaining parameters are a confidence level and a window; and judge speed by a ratio (3DMark's worst-to-best 97%, or the coefficient of variation). A maximum time and a "not stable" label stay needed (Barrett et al.). Open: which test works best on a 1 °C-resolution sensor with fan cycling; not compared.
- **Earlier synthesis (2026-11-17, kept for the record; its band rule failed in the 2026-11-18 runs):** a fixed 10 minutes per setting measures a plateau that is reached in a few minutes. Start with a minimum (90 s) and extend only until stable: stable when the GPU temperature varies by at most 1 °C over the last 60 s and tokens/s varies by at most 2% (coefficient of variation) over the last 5 requests; stop at a maximum of 8 minutes and record "not stable". Measure over that stable window, with no separate measuring phase. Run the settings from the lowest clock to the highest, so the laptop only ever heats up between settings and needs no rest. On the checked laptop this is expected to take about 2 minutes a setting (about 10 minutes for four), against about 55 for a fixed 10 minutes each. The first run after a change (new driver, cleaned fans, new paste) is worth a full 20 minutes, to confirm the plateau.

## Limits

The 2026-11-17 stable-window thresholds (1 °C over 60 s, 2% over 5 requests, 90 s minimum, 8 minutes maximum) were the checker's choice, not from a source; the 2026-11-18 runs showed the 1 °C band unworkable on this laptop. The environmental-test figures come from secondary summaries; MIL-STD-810, IEC 60068 and IEC 62368 were not read. The Cao-Rhinehart and Dickey-Fuller methods were read through abstracts and a tutorial summary, not tried on this data. The Georges method was read through a secondary summary.

The warm-up lengths come from one review site, one laptop maker and one blog; none is a laptop standard. SPEC's power benchmark rules were not found. The plateau time is one measurement on one laptop, at a capped clock.
