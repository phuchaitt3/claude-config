# Motivation answers on application forms

- **Question:** How much weight does a free-text "why do you want to join" answer carry in screening, and why do many of them read as hollow compared with what an interview can cover?
- **Finding:**
  - **No measured weight was found.** Career and recruiting sites say the question tests motivation, preparation and fit. None gives a measured effect on screening outcomes. Two figures that circulate ("AI screens this answer first in 87% of companies", "65% of candidates are rejected for generic answers") are unsourced marketing claims; do not use them.
  - **Generic answers are a known negative signal.** Several recruiting sources say recruiters hear the same flattery and templates constantly, and that a generic reason ("a great opportunity") shows no research or thought. Advice repeated across them: name something specific about the organisation and tie it to what you can do or want to build. One source adds that a canned answer is more likely because candidates have heard the question many times.
  - **The closest evidence on the cover-letter-like text is a split.** Surveys of hiring managers disagree widely on whether such text is read at all: from 26% reading it always or often (ResumeBuilder, 948 hiring managers, 2024) to 83% reading most of it (Resume Genius, 625 hiring managers). Most of these surveys come from resume-writing companies that sell related services. This is the nearest proxy found, since no survey asks about form-based motivation answers specifically.
  - *Checker's inference:* why such answers read as hollow. A motivation answer is a claim about oneself with nothing to check, and many applicants write the same one; a project or skills answer can be checked against the CV and probed in an interview. So the form text is a low-information signal, and an interview, where follow-up questions test the same claim, can cover it in more depth. This is reasoning from the sources above, not a finding any of them states. It also does not say the answer can be skipped: a required field left generic can still count against the candidate.
- **Evidence strength:** *anecdotal* for the generic-answer point (career blogs and recruiter sites agree, no study); *sample or survey* for the cover-letter split, with the vendor bias noted. *No data found* on the weight of a form motivation answer in screening.

- **Limits:** Three standard searches; pages were read through search summaries, not in full. No source studies form-based motivation answers, residency or graduate programmes, or Vietnamese employers; everything found is general career advice, mostly US. A search for structured-interview validity of motivation questions returned nothing on that point, so how well a motivation answer predicts performance is open. The "why hollow" reasoning is the checker's inference.
