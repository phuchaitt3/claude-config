# Laptop temperature tiers for long unattended jobs

- **Question:** Which GPU temperatures are normal, worth flagging or dangerous on a laptop running long jobs, what wears the laptop out over time, and is a steady load for 8-10 hours a night (GPU around 75 °C) a good idea?
- **Checked:** 2026-11-14
- **Evidence strength:** *anecdotal* for what the nvidia-smi temperature fields mean (NVIDIA developer forum replies; NVIDIA's own manual was not found); *vendor or single source* for battery care (Lenovo user guide, Battery University); *common practice* for the Arrhenius rule of thumb, with its sources disputing how far it applies; *sample or survey* for field data on temperature and failures (datacenter disks and DRAM, not GPUs); *no data found* for laptop GPU lifetime against hours at a given temperature.

## Finding

- **Read the card's own limits; don't use a generic number.** `nvidia-smi -q -d TEMPERATURE` lists them. Forum replies on NVIDIA's developer forum say: *Target Temperature* is the set point the fan control aims to hold; *Slowdown Temp* is where the clocks throttle; *Shutdown Temp* is where the GPU turns off. What *Max Operating Temp* means is disputed. Notebook GPUs often have a fixed target that can't be changed. One guide adds: when the laptop maker publishes a lower limit than the GPU vendor, use the lower one.
- **Tiers (checker's synthesis from the above, not a published scale):** below Target = normal; at or near Target for long stretches = working as designed, but the fan is at its limit; any thermal-slowdown time (`clocks_event_reasons` thermal bits, or a non-zero `SW/HW Thermal Slowdown` counter) or a reading at Slowdown Temp = throttling, which costs speed and makes timing runs unreliable; Shutdown Temp = protection trip. Generic guides put the usual laptop GPU throttle point around 90 °C, and say under 85 °C is safe for desktop cards. These are secondary sources.
- **Long-term wear from heat is real but not quantified for GPUs.** The "every 10 °C halves lifetime" rule comes from the Arrhenius equation. It fits some failure mechanisms (electromigration, corrosion), with an activation-energy-dependent factor of about 2 per 10 °C over roughly 75-125 °C, and not others. Electronics Cooling and TI both call it a rule of thumb. TI's industrial parts are rated for 10 years at 105 °C junction. No GPU-specific wear data was found. Checker's inference: running cooler extends life by an unknown amount, and a running record of temperature is what lets a trend (dust, dried paste) show up.
- **Sustained load for 8-10 hours a night: no source measures it for laptops.** Distance from the throttle and shutdown points only says the chip is protected in the short term; it says nothing about wear, so "below the target" is not the same as "harmless for years". What the sources do say:
  - *Silicon:* field data from Google, LANL and SciNet (El-Sayed, Schroeder et al., USENIX ;login: 2013; about 70,000 disks for sector errors, 200,000 for replacements, DRAM across five platforms) found failure rates rose with temperature much more slowly than Arrhenius predicts, often linearly or not at all, and that **temperature variability had a more pronounced and consistent effect than average temperature** on disk sector errors. That is datacenter hardware at 20-60 °C, not a GPU at 75 °C.
  - *Solder joints:* fatigue is driven by heat-cool cycles (Coffin-Manson); steady temperature acts through creep instead. An Intel study that recorded a laptop's temperature history for three months found the real cycle profile, not only the range, sets BGA fatigue life. Checker's inference, untested for laptops: a rule that pauses jobs to cool down adds heat-cool cycles, so it can trade one wear mechanism for another; a steady moderate temperature may be kinder than repeated cool-downs.
  - *Fans, paste and dust wear first* under continuous load, per mining-laptop articles (anecdotal). Fan bearing life is rated as L10 hours (90% still working): about 60,000-70,000 h for ball bearings at up to 50 °C air, about half that for sleeve bearings (engineering forum and vendor notes; laptop fan ratings are rarely published). Checker's arithmetic: 8-10 h a night is about 3,000-3,650 h a year.
  - *Hard disks:* Google's 2007 disk study found high temperature hurt only at about 40 °C and above, and only in drives at least 3 years old (secondary report). A laptop with its data on an internal HDD should log the disk's temperature too (SMART; on Windows it needs administrator rights or a tool such as LibreHardwareMonitor).
  - Checker's summary: the levers that cut wear are lower power (a quieter power mode or a power cap lowers temperature and fan speed together), clean vents and airflow under the chassis, steady rather than stop-start load, and a trend log: the same load running hotter month after month points to dust or dried paste.
- **The battery is the part heat wears out fastest.** Heat combined with a full charge oxidises the cells irreversibly (Battery University; a 2024 RSC Advances study on cells stored fully charged at 45 °C). Battery University says a laptop's interior reaches about 45 °C in use, and that life expectancy at high temperature is half that at 20 °C. Lenovo recommends conservation mode for a laptop that stays plugged in; it caps the charge (75-80% on some Legion models per Lenovo's guide, about 55-60% on others per Lenovo support and user reports). It is switched in Lenovo Vantage, PC Manager or Legion Zone.

## Limits

NVIDIA's nvidia-smi manual was not read, so the field meanings rest on forum replies. No source gives a laptop GPU's lifetime as a function of temperature or of hours under load; the field data on temperature and failures is datacenter disks and DRAM, and the GPU-lifespan figures found (a secondhand Google quote of 1-3 years for datacenter GPUs; Meta's Llama 3 failure counts) concern datacenter accelerators at full utilisation, not laptops. Battery figures come from cells and general guides, not from measurements on a specific laptop model. CPU limits are in `findings/laptop-cpu-thermal-limit`.
